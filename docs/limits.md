# What to know before you trust it

Voss is a character and a reference tool for health information and education. It can suggest possible explanations for symptoms, with sources, but it does not make diagnoses, treat or prescribe, and it is not a substitute for a doctor, pharmacist or emergency service. It can be wrong. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

A figure a tool worked out carries the tag `[derived]`, and the line says what it was worked out from. A figure that was handed to the tool says who handed it over ("printed on the report, as given to the tool", "counted by the person"). This page is the longer answer, command by command.

Three things hold for all of it:

- **A tool holds the form; the assistant does the thinking.** Where a command is given something to write down (an explanation, what a photo shows, what a letter asks for), it checks the shape and the rules and nothing else. It cannot tell whether what it was given is true.
- **Nothing runs by itself.** There is no timer and no notification. A weekly round, a seasonal reminder or a check-in happens inside a conversation, when the assistant runs the command, or not at all. What can bring you back to one sits outside the toolkit: a calendar file that `remind` writes, or a scheduled task or a message where the assistant's platform can set one.
- **A copy grows old.** Every database is a copy made on a day it names. `boot` and `sources` say what is installed and how old it is, and a file past its limit says so before it answers.

## The data

- **Seven databases come with him: one explains words, one explains medical tests, one reads codes, one grades pairs of medicines, one holds American product labels, one holds a few passages of published guidance, and one holds first aid as Wikipedia has it.** Each is optional, and a command whose file is missing says that nothing is installed. None of them is about any one country: for a country's services, its law and the medicines sold there Voss looks things up with whatever the assistant can reach and cites them, or says he has no data.
- **A passage is its source's, and as old as the day it was copied.** `pet`, `kit`, `desk`, `season` and `firstaid` print short passages word for word: FDA on what may be dangerous to a pet, nidirect and the Health and Safety Executive on first-aid kits, the Health and Safety Executive on breaks from a screen, ECDC on when the flu vaccination is given, Wikipedia on first aid. Each was written for its own readers: pet owners in the United States, employers in Great Britain, households in Northern Ireland, health authorities in Europe. None is a rule for you, and none of those bodies has anything to do with this skill.
- **Some things are in no file at all**: a first-aid body's own guideline, what the law asks for in a car's kit, a figure for water, any country's vaccination dates, advice for heat and cold, help for carers, reference ranges for lab tests, anything on food and medicines, product information from outside the United States, and how care is arranged in any country. The commands say so each time.
- **`sources` reports; it does not audit.** It prints what each file says about itself: source, address, licence, the day it was retrieved, and its age against a limit. For ICD-11 the limit is "when WHO publishes a new release", which no tool can see from here.

## Triage and the clinic

- **`triage` is a checklist, not a judgement.** It prints the four levels and the red flags, word for word from `references/clinical-rules.md`. The list of red flags is not complete, and it says so.
- **Emergency numbers are passed on, not vouched for.** `triage --country` prints what Wikidata lists for a country, with what each number is for where Wikidata says. Nobody checked them one by one, a country may be missing, and a number marked for one service may reach others too. Inside the European Union 112 is put first, because the European Commission says it reaches every emergency service there. If you know your number, use it; if you do not and it is urgent, do not wait for a tool.
- **`assess` keeps the form of a careful guess; the guess is the assistant's.** It holds no medical knowledge. It is handed the possible explanations and refuses a record that breaks the clinical rules: one with no must-not-miss, with a percentage for a confidence, with an explanation that names no source, or with a phrase such as "probably nothing". It cannot tell whether an explanation is right, whether a source named exists or says what is claimed, or whether the triage level it was given was the right one. "General knowledge" counts as a source when it is said to be that. At level 1 it prints no explanations at all: the number comes first. How good the reasoning is depends on the model behind Voss, and a real doctor can examine you, which nothing here can.
- **`look` does not see the photo.** The assistant looks; the tool is given a description and a level. It moves the level up by one for five signs (a changing mole, spreading redness, red streaks, blackened skin, anything on an infant) when it is told of one, and cannot notice a sign it was not told of. A photo shows colour and size badly, and shows nothing of heat, pain or how fast a thing is changing. The tool does not keep the picture; what the platform you are using keeps is up to the platform.
- **`firstaid` holds no first-aid guideline.** It prints the emergency number on file for the country, or says to lead with the local one, and then what Wikipedia's articles say about CPR, the recovery position, bleeding, burns or choking, at revisions that were read before they went in. Wikipedia is an encyclopaedia that anyone can edit. Its account of what first-aid bodies teach is not a guideline, parts of it are dated, and the sections that were unsound were left out, so it is not complete either. The coaching is the assistant's, in short lines, and nothing checks that it keeps to the passages or to a current guideline. The operator on the line outranks all of it. It is no substitute for the call, and none for a first-aid course.
- **`term` repeats a source; it does not examine anyone.** It prints the MedlinePlus summary of a health topic, or a short definition, in the source's own words, with the day the file was retrieved. The file is a copy, as old as that day: MedlinePlus itself is the current one, and a copy older than a year says so before it answers. The summaries were written for readers in the United States, so they name 911 and American services. A figure in a summary is general, and a lab value is still read against the range on your own report. When several entries could be meant it lists them and does not pick; when nothing matches it says "nothing on file", which is a gap in the file and not a fact about the word. Only the summaries are in it: not the pages MedlinePlus links to, not its encyclopedia, not its drug pages.
- **`code` reads a code back; it does not code anything.** It prints the title the World Health Organization gives an ICD-11 code, where the code sits in the classification and what is under it, character for character from the yearly release named in the output. The release file holds codes, titles and structure, and no descriptions: what a title means in practice is Voss's own explanation, said as his. It is ICD-11 only. A code from ICD-10, which much paperwork still uses, is not in it, and there is no table between the two, because WHO's licence keeps such tables for a written agreement. `--find` looks for words in the titles and nothing else: a title that holds your words is not a diagnosis, and WHO's wording may not be yours. A code on a letter is the doctor's; the tool cannot tell whether it was the right one.
- **`visit` is general advice.** Its lists were written for this skill. No database is behind them, and nothing in them is about your complaint.
- **`decode` writes down what the assistant read in a letter.** The tool does not read the letter: it is handed the things to do, the new medicines and whatever looked odd. A date read wrongly goes into the calendar wrongly, so check each against the paper. It never contradicts the doctor: what looks inconsistent is marked "worth confirming with your doctor or pharmacist", and that is all. Calendar entries can be read by anyone who can see the calendar (`--discreet` leaves out what they are for). The letter itself is not kept.
- **`myth` records a verdict; it does not reach one.** The verdict is the assistant's, one of four. The tool wants at least one source, each with its date, unless the verdict is "no good evidence either way". It does not check that a source exists or says what is claimed. A verdict is about the claim, not about what you should do.
- **`teach` is general knowledge, and says so.** No database is behind a lesson, though `term` may have a topic on the subject. A lesson is about bodies in general and never about yours.
- **`find` holds nothing about any country.** It lists what there is to establish (the number, where to go at night and at weekends, what the emergency department is for, an advice line, what a patient is owed, where old medicines go) and tells Voss to look it up on the country's own official pages or in its law, and to say which page and when. What comes back is as good as the lookup, and as current as the page. Whether you need the night clinic or an ambulance is a triage question, and no list answers it.
- **`staykit` is general advice for a planned stay in hospital**, written for this skill. It is no hospital's list, and where the ward sends its own, that one comes first. It says nothing about getting ready for a procedure (what to stop eating, which medicines to pause): that comes from the ward and nobody else. It holds nothing on a patient's rights anywhere: those are looked up for where the hospital is, and cited.
- **`pet` records how soon a vet should hear of it, and the reading is not a vet's.** "Now", "today" or "tomorrow" is the assistant's call, and the tool says so; anything that may be a poison is always "now". With a poison in question it prints what FDA lists as potentially dangerous to a pet: the more common foods, household things and plants, written in 2023 for readers in the United States. The page says itself that how toxic a thing is depends on the animal and on how much it ate, and something that is not on the list is not thereby harmless. No animal medicine of any kind is on file. There is never a dose for an animal, of anything. The weight on file is for the vet.

## Lab results

- **`labs` knows no reference range.** It reads the value you give against the range you give, which should be the one printed on your own report, and says which side of it the value falls on. It does not turn a result into a diagnosis. `labs --about` prints MedlinePlus's page on a test: what it is, what it is used for, what happens during it. That page was written for readers in the United States. It is about the test and not about you, and where it speaks of high or low results, the range on your own report is the one that counts. A test is found by its name or by another name the page gives; the tool says what it read the words as, and lists the tests when several fit. A value the lab printed as "<0.5" is treated as exactly that: where it cannot be placed against the range, the tool says so. If the lab's flag and the printed range disagree, it asks you to read the report again.
- **`result` and `labread` keep figures as they were given.** The tool cannot know whether a figure was typed or read correctly. With `labread` the reading of the photo is the assistant's, and the read-back is the safeguard: check every row against the paper before you say yes. A figure it could not read is marked unreadable and left out, never guessed. One call takes one report, with one date and one lab, and sixty rows at most. A result that is not a plain number ("negative", "<0.5") is kept as printed.
- **`trend` sets each result against the range of its own report, and never two reports on one scale.** It says when the unit, the lab or the printed range changed between two results, because then the figures cannot simply be set side by side. It does not say whether a change matters: that is a question for the doctor, and the tool says so. It knows a test by the name it was stored under, so "TSH" and "Thyrotropin" are two tests to it. A result stored with no range has nothing read from it.

## Medicines

- **`med` reads a label out; it does not prescribe from it.** The labels are the ones companies have filed with the United States Food and Drug Administration. FDA says of them that their content "has neither been altered nor verified by FDA" and that they "may not be the labeling on currently distributed products or identical to the labeling that is approved". They are American: the same substance sold where you live may come in other strengths, for other uses, with other warnings, under another name. So the leaflet in your own pack is the authority, and where it differs from what `med` prints, yours is right. One label is kept for each substance, type and route, out of many: another company's label for the same medicine may word things differently. There is no dose in the file, on purpose, and no account of how a medicine works or how it was studied. A brand name is found only if it is an American one. One name can be on packs of quite different medicines: a shop's "Pain Relief" may be paracetamol, ibuprofen or a numbing cream, and well-known brands sell several things under one name. Then `med` lists them and picks none, because only the active ingredient printed on your own pack says which it is. The tool says what it read a name as, and says so when it used only part of what was typed: if that is not your medicine, the answer is not about your medicine. A few hundred packs that hold two kinds of tablet are missing, because FDA's data does not say what is in them. A product sold outside the United States may be missing as well, or may be another medicine under the same name: for those Voss looks the product up in that country's register of medicines and cites it. What a label says a medicine is for is not advice to take it.
- **`sidefx` searches a label for a word.** It looks through the label's side effects, warnings, boxed warning and text for patients for the words you give, and prints the sentences that hold them. It does not know that "itching" is "pruritus" on a label: try both. It cannot tell you that the medicine caused your symptom, and a symptom that is not on the label is not ruled out. Frequencies are the label's, from the company's own studies. Triage still comes first, and whether to carry on with a medicine is for the prescriber or the pharmacist.
- **`interact` and `watch` pass on a grade; they do not judge a combination.** They read pairs of medicines and the grade DDInter 2.0 gives each pair (Major, Moderate, Minor, or none). That is all DDInter lets anyone download, so there is no account of why two medicines interact and none of what to do about it: that question goes to a pharmacist or a prescriber, with the grade. Three things make "not listed" worthless as reassurance, and the tools say so every time. The file is partial: a pair is in it only if one of its two medicines belongs to certain groups of the ATC classification, so two medicines for the heart, two for the nervous system or two against infections are not there at all. Where the files prove that both medicines are outside those groups, the pair is reported as "out of reach" and not as unlisted; where they cannot prove it, an unlisted pair may still be one the file could never have held. It knows nothing of doses, of food, drink or herbs it cannot name, or of the person's kidneys, liver, age or pregnancy. And DDInter itself warns that the absence of an interaction does not mean there is none. Its makers read the literature up to May 2023, so nothing found out since is in it. A grade is about two substances, not about you. Names are a weak point: a medicine is found under DDInter's name or under another name taken from Wikidata, matched by spelling and not checked one by one. The output says what each name was read as; if that is not your medicine, the answer is not about your medicine. A brand name it does not know is reported as unknown and is checked against nothing.
- **`schedule` repeats a schedule it was given.** It never sets one, never tidies one, and does not know whether a dose is right. Dose times are written into the calendar without a time zone, so 08:00 stays 08:00 where you are; a calendar application that insists on a zone will use its own. That means the reminders follow the phone's clock when you travel: right for most tablets, wrong for anything taken at a strict interval, so ask the pharmacist or the prescriber how to carry such a medicine across time zones. Anyone who can see the calendar can read the entries, and a phone may show them on its lock screen: `--discreet` leaves the names out. The file is never written into a git working tree or into the skill's own folder.
- **`refill` is arithmetic on that schedule.** It assumes every scheduled dose was taken, from the day the pack was opened and counting that whole day, because it cannot know otherwise. Missed doses, extra doses and a second pack are invisible to it. Where the sums do not fit (the pack should have run out already) it says so and stops. It uses this machine's clock unless you pass `--now`, and a remote machine's clock may be hours from yours. When it matters, count the pack.
- **`boxread` compares; it does not identify.** The assistant reads the box, and the tool sets the name beside the American labels: what substance they know that name as, and whether it is the one read off the box. Those are American brand names. A name can be another medicine elsewhere, and a name they do not know, or know on packs of different medicines, is compared with nothing. So a mismatch is a reason to read the box again, not a finding. The strength is written down as it was read and checked against nothing. A part that could not be read is never guessed, and the box in your hand wins over any file.
- **`cabinet` counts days; it knows nothing about a medicine.** An expiry date is the maker's, and one given as a month runs to the last day of that month. A medicine that got too warm or too damp may be spoilt long before its date, and the tool cannot know. "Open too long" is only worked out where the pack's own label said how long it keeps once opened, and that was stored. "Twice" means the American labels know two names on the list as the same substance. A name they do not know, or know on packs of different medicines, is compared with nothing, and the tool lists it as not checked; and since a brand can be another medicine outside the United States, the active ingredient printed on each pack is what settles it. Nothing is on file about where old medicines are taken back. The cabinet is the household's list, not one person's: everybody who uses the file sees all of it, with whose each medicine is, so a medicine nobody else should know of does not belong on it.

## What you keep, and the pages made from it

- **`diary` keeps your words.** The date, the symptom, how bad you rated it from 0 to 10, what helped. Nothing in it is checked, weighed or interpreted, and a pattern in it is a question for a doctor.
- **`reading` keeps figures; it judges none.** A home reading is stored as you read it, and a run of them is shown with the lowest, the highest and the latest. For a reading of two figures, such as blood pressure, each figure is told apart: the lowest of one and the lowest of the other need not come from the same day. There is no threshold in the tool, and the target on file is in your doctor's words: nothing compares a reading with it. Whether the cuff fits, whether you sat still, whether the meter is right, the tool cannot know. A reading that worries you, or comes with symptoms, is not a matter for a log.
- **`followup` remembers what you agreed to be asked about.** It lists those, and the visits on file from the last fortnight. It does not know whether you went. Whether he asks, and asks only once, is his rule to keep. An entry is removed with `forget` once it has been asked and answered.
- **Nothing is saved that was not read back.** `diary`, `result`, `labread`, `reading`, `followup`, `cabinet`, `kit` and `pet` print what they would store and a code, and write only when the same command comes back with that code; a different change, a changed file or another day gives another code. That makes the read-back hard to skip. It cannot make anyone listen to it.
- **`card`, `passport` and `brief` print what is on file, in your words.** The headings are in English. A second language beside them is the assistant's translation, which no tool checks, and it is nobody's official form. An entry stays as you gave it, so a doctor abroad may not be able to read it. A page is not a medical record, and its last line says so. It is written only after you have seen exactly that page, and never into a git working tree or into the skill's own folder.
- **`timetable` prints the schedule on file, and sets nothing.** It lists each medicine under the times it is taken, with how it is taken as you told him. A medicine with no times on file is left off, or listed under "No set time" if you ask. If the schedule on file is wrong or out of date, so is the page: it carries the day it was made. Anyone who sees it on the fridge can read which medicines you take.
- **`note` writes down what the assistant gives it.** The lines are the assistant's own account of the conversation. The tool refuses a phrase Voss never says, and checks nothing else: not that a line was really said, and not that it is right. It is not a medical record, it is not kept by him, and its last line says that a real doctor outranks it.
- **Anyone who is handed a page can read it.** So can anyone who picks up a phone with a medical ID on its lock screen. That is what such a page is for, and why you choose each line. The tool writes a text file laid out for the phone's own emergency screen; putting it there is yours to do.
- **`kit` lists what you told it is in a kit.** It flags what has expired by the dates you gave, and prints two published lists of what a kit may hold: nidirect's for a home, and the Health and Safety Executive's for a workplace with low hazards. Both call themselves suggestions, and they differ: one names pain relievers, the other advises against keeping tablets in the box. What any country's law asks for in a car is not on file.
- **`carer` shows the records of the people in someone's care.** The note on consent is what was written down; the tool cannot know whether the person really agreed, or still does. A record with no questions of its own is guarded by the carer's gate, which is a soft lock. Nothing is on file about help for carers.
- **`round` is a list of what is due, for those who switched it on.** Refills by the schedule's arithmetic (with `refill`'s assumption that every dose was taken), the visits on file, expiry dates from the cabinet and the kits, and what has gone stale. It leaves out the things a person keeps to themselves, so that a round can be read where others see it. It is off unless switched on in the person's file, or asked for. "Weekly" is a habit, not a timer: `remind` can put it in a calendar.
- **`remind` writes a calendar file and the words of an invitation; it sends nothing.** The entries repeat on your own calendar (a weekly round on the day you chose, a seasonal check on the first of March, June, September and December, a check-in as often as you said), carry no time zone, and say only that it is time to talk to Voss. Imported twice, the file gives each entry twice on some calendars, and an entry repeats until you delete it. Where the assistant's platform can schedule a task, set a reminder or send a message, Voss may offer that with the same words: whether it runs is the platform's doing, a task that runs with nobody there cannot pass your gate and so never opens your file, and an e-mail is not private. Nothing from a personal file goes into a reminder.
- **`desk`, `season` and `checkin` are notices a person has to switch on.** Off means none is given. `desk` prints what the Health and Safety Executive says about breaks from a screen, which was written for people at work in Great Britain; no figure is on file for water or posture. `season` prints what ECDC says about when the flu vaccination is given, which is about Europe and names no country's dates; nothing is on file about the weather. For the rest the tool tells Voss to cite what he looks up or leave it out. "One notice, and no more" is his rule to keep: the tool does not count. A check-in is a question and a listen. It is not a screening, it has no score, and any sign of crisis goes to the clinical rules at once.
- **`translate` checks figures, not meaning.** The translating is the assistant's. The tool compares the figures of the original with those of the translation, as written, and counts the terms it was told to keep. It is strict on purpose: a decimal comma turned into a point, or a date written another way, counts as a change. It cannot tell a wrong word from a right one, or notice a missing "not". A number written out in words is not a figure to it. It does not print the figures themselves, because one may be somebody's result. It works on text: a scan has to be read first, and what was read wrongly is compared wrongly.
- **`recipe` and `scale` are baking, not advice.** The twelve recipes, one for each month by the seasons north of the equator, were written for this skill and nobody has baked them from this file yet: they say "untested" until someone has. `recipe --for` sets a recipe beside the allergies and intolerances on file by matching words: it can miss one, it lists what it could not match, and it never says that a recipe is safe for anyone. The allergens are read off the list of ingredients, which is no promise about a kitchen. `scale` multiplies weights exactly; the tin and the baking time are for the recipe as written and do not scale with it.

## The personal file

- **The gate is a soft lock.** `ignition` judges a reply against the answers on file, forgiving case, accents, word order and small typos, and never prints one. It judges and does nothing more: the other commands do not check that it was passed, nothing counts failed attempts, and nothing stops an assistant from opening the file. So keeping a file shut until then, keeping to three questions, and not looking are Voss's rules to follow. That stops a file being read aloud to the wrong person in a conversation. It is not encryption: the file is plain JSON, and anyone who can open the package can read it. Once passed, a gate stays open for the conversation.
- **Nobody is turned away at the gate.** Someone who does not pass it gets the same care and the same knowledge, only without the personal file. There is no way round the gate for an emergency, and none is needed: the emergency answer is the number to call, and it never depended on the file.
- **`profile` reads back what you said, as you said it.** Ages are worked out from the date of birth on the day you ask; a year alone leaves a year's doubt, and the line says so. "Stale" notes (a pregnancy status over thirty days old, medicines told over a year ago, travel over three months ago) are reminders to ask again. A weight has no date that runs out, and an old profile is not flagged as a whole. Nothing is deleted for being old. `boot` shows the day the file was last changed, which also tells you whether the copy that is loaded is the one you saved last.
- **`intake` and `rebuild` store only what was read back.** The code `intake` prints stands for exactly the file that would result; a different change, a changed file or a different day gives a different code, and `rebuild` then refuses. That makes the read-back hard to skip. It cannot make anyone listen to it.
- **The check for numbers that are never stored is a net, not a promise.** It recognises payment card numbers, IBANs, US social security numbers written with their hyphens, and two national number formats by their check digits, plus a few tell-tale words. A number in another shape will get past it. Refusing those is still Voss's rule.
- **`forget` removes from the new file only.** The field, entry or profile is gone from the file it writes: no empty slot, no note that it was there. The package or file you used until then still holds it, and so do backups and anything already said in a conversation. Delete the old copies yourself.
- **`rebuild` writes a private file.** A rebuilt package has your personal file in it, and the household file if your copy has one. The tool refuses to write it into a git working tree or into the package it is running from. Where you keep it after that is up to you.

## The toolkit itself

- **`boot` shows this machine's clock**, which may not be yours: a remote machine can be hours away. `refill` and `round` take `--now` for that reason. Everything else that counts days (an age, an expiry, a stale note) counts them on the machine's date.
- **`theatre` prints a banner and the three guardrails.** Switching mode changes nothing in the tools. That real medicines keep their real rules on stage, and that a real symptom ends the scene, are his rules to keep.
- **`tools --find` matches words.** It looks for your words in what each command says it does and in a few more words listed for it. It can miss, or name the wrong command: `tools` lists them all.
- **`check --reply` checks form.** Typography, the marker line, ear cues, a banner written wrongly, a few phrases that are never said, and length. It cannot tell whether a claim is sourced, whether the tone suits the person, or whether a reply is kind.
- **`selftest` checks the toolkit on this machine**: known answers for the date sums, the refill sums, the lab comparison, the calendar format and the gate; that the data files and pictures are present; that each database says where it came from and that its packed text can be read by this Python; and that the personal file, if there is one, reads and has nothing to fix. It says where a problem is and never quotes what is written there.
- **Voss himself is fiction.** The android, the fur and the cyan eyes are a character, and so are his senses: he has not seen you, listened to your chest or felt your pulse, and he does not say that he has. What he knows of you is what you told him and what you showed him. The rules he follows are real.
