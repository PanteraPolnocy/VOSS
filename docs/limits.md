# What to know before you trust it

Voss is a character and a reference tool for health information and education. It can suggest possible explanations for symptoms, with sources, but it does not make diagnoses, treat or prescribe, and it is not a substitute for a doctor, pharmacist or emergency service. It can be wrong. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

A figure a tool worked out carries the tag `[derived]`, and the line says what it was worked out from. A figure that was handed to the tool says who handed it over ("printed on the report, as given to the tool", "counted by the person"). This page is the longer answer, command by command.

Three things hold for all of it:

- **A tool holds the form; the assistant does the thinking.** Where a command is given something to write down (an explanation, what a photo shows, what a letter asks for), it checks the shape and the rules and nothing else. It cannot tell whether what it was given is true.
- **Nothing runs by itself.** There is no timer and no notification. A weekly round, a seasonal reminder or a check-in happens inside a conversation, when the assistant runs the command, or not at all.
- **A copy grows old.** Every database is a copy made on a day it names. `boot` and `sources` say what is installed and how old it is, and a file past its limit says so before it answers.

## The data

- **Five databases are in: one explains words, one reads codes, one grades pairs of medicines, one holds American product labels, and one is a pack for Poland.** Each is optional, and a command whose file is missing says that nothing is installed. No other country has a pack: for anything about another country's medicines and services Voss looks things up with whatever the assistant can reach and cites them, or says he has no data.
- **Some things are in no file at all**: a first-aid guideline, what poisons a pet, what a first-aid kit should hold, figures for screen breaks, seasonal dates, help for carers, reference ranges for lab tests, anything on food and medicines, and product information from outside the United States. No source was found for them whose terms allow its content to be passed on, or, for the ranges, none would be right. The commands say so each time.
- **`sources` reports; it does not audit.** It prints what each file says about itself: source, address, licence, the day it was retrieved, and its age against a limit. The limits are proposals. For ICD-11 the limit is "when WHO publishes a new release", which no tool can see from here.

## Triage and the clinic

- **`triage` is a checklist, not a judgement.** It prints the four levels and the red flags, word for word from `references/clinical-rules.md`. The list of red flags is not complete, and it says so.
- **Emergency numbers are passed on, not vouched for.** `triage --country` prints what Wikidata lists for a country, with what each number is for where Wikidata says. Nobody checked them one by one, a country may be missing, and a number marked for one service may reach others too. Inside the European Union 112 is put first, because the European Commission says it reaches every emergency service there. With the Poland pack installed, a second line gives Poland's own numbers from its numbering plan, which is the law's own word on what each number is for. If you know your number, use it; if you do not and it is urgent, do not wait for a tool.
- **`assess` keeps the form of a careful guess; the guess is the assistant's.** It holds no medical knowledge. It is handed the possible explanations and refuses a record that breaks the clinical rules: one with no must-not-miss, with a percentage for a confidence, with an explanation that names no source, or with a phrase such as "probably nothing". It cannot tell whether an explanation is right, whether a source named exists or says what is claimed, or whether the triage level it was given was the right one. "General knowledge" counts as a source when it is said to be that. At level 1 it prints no explanations at all: the number comes first. How good the reasoning is depends on the model behind Voss, and a real doctor can examine you, which nothing here can.
- **`look` does not see the photo.** The assistant looks; the tool is given a description and a level. It moves the level up by one for five signs (a changing mole, spreading redness, red streaks, blackened skin, anything on an infant) when it is told of one, and cannot notice a sign it was not told of. A photo shows colour and size badly, and shows nothing of heat, pain or how fast a thing is changing. The tool does not keep the picture; what the platform you are using keeps is up to the platform.
- **`firstaid` holds no first-aid guideline.** It prints the emergency number on file for the country, or says to lead with the local one, and then the frame: short lines, one action each, and the operator's instructions before his. The steps are the assistant's, from a published guideline it has to name with its year. Nothing checks that it did, or that the steps are right. It is no substitute for the call, and none for a first-aid course.
- **`term` repeats a source; it does not examine anyone.** It prints the MedlinePlus summary of a health topic, or a short definition, in the source's own words, with the day the file was retrieved. The file is a copy, as old as that day: MedlinePlus itself is the current one, and a copy older than six months says so before it answers. The summaries were written for readers in the United States, so they name 911 and American services. A figure in a summary is general, and a lab value is still read against the range on your own report. When several entries could be meant it lists them and does not pick; when nothing matches it says "nothing on file", which is a gap in the file and not a fact about the word. Only the summaries are in it: not the pages MedlinePlus links to, not its encyclopedia, not its drug pages.
- **`code` reads a code back; it does not code anything.** It prints the title the World Health Organization gives an ICD-11 code, where the code sits in the classification and what is under it, character for character from the yearly release named in the output. The release file holds codes, titles and structure, and no descriptions: what a title means in practice is Voss's own explanation, said as his. It is ICD-11 only. A code from ICD-10, which much paperwork still uses, is not in it, and there is no table between the two, because WHO's licence keeps such tables for a written agreement. `--find` looks for words in the titles and nothing else: a title that holds your words is not a diagnosis, and WHO's wording may not be yours. A code on a letter is the doctor's; the tool cannot tell whether it was the right one.
- **`visit` is general advice.** Its lists were written for this skill. No database is behind them, and nothing in them is about your complaint.
- **`decode` writes down what the assistant read in a letter.** The tool does not read the letter: it is handed the things to do, the new medicines and whatever looked odd. A date read wrongly goes into the calendar wrongly, so check each against the paper. It never contradicts the doctor: what looks inconsistent is marked "worth confirming with your doctor or pharmacist", and that is all. Calendar entries can be read by anyone who can see the calendar (`--discreet` leaves out what they are for). The letter itself is not kept.
- **`myth` records a verdict; it does not reach one.** The verdict is the assistant's, one of four. The tool wants at least one source, each with its date, unless the verdict is "no good evidence either way". It does not check that a source exists or says what is claimed. A verdict is about the claim, not about what you should do.
- **`teach` is general knowledge, and says so.** No database is behind a lesson, though `term` may have a topic on the subject. A lesson is about bodies in general and never about yours.
- **`find` tells how care is arranged in Poland; it does not tell you where to go.** Ten facts, each in the skill's own words with what it rests on underneath: the law, quoted in Polish by article, or the official page it was read on. Where a law is published only as a PDF it was read by a person on the day named, and nothing checks it again by itself. Opening hours and telephone numbers were true on the page on the day it was read; they change. The reading of the law is the skill's, and it is not legal advice. Waiting times are not in it at all, because they change every week. Whether you need the night clinic or an ambulance is a triage question, and the tool leaves it there.
- **`staykit` is general advice for a planned stay in hospital**, written for this skill. It is no hospital's list, and where the ward sends its own, that one comes first. It says nothing about getting ready for a procedure (what to stop eating, which medicines to pause): that comes from the ward and nobody else. The part on a patient's rights is from the Poland pack, for Poland.
- **`pet` records how soon a vet should hear of it, and the reading is not a vet's.** "Now", "today" or "tomorrow" is the assistant's call, and the tool says so; anything that may be a poison is always "now". No list of what poisons an animal is on file, and no animal medicine of any kind. There is never a dose for an animal, of anything. The weight on file is for the vet.

## Lab results

- **`labs` knows no reference range.** It reads the value you give against the range you give, which should be the one printed on your own report, and says which side of it the value falls on. It does not know what the test measures, and it does not turn a result into a diagnosis. A value the lab printed as "<0.5" is treated as exactly that: where it cannot be placed against the range, the tool says so. If the lab's flag and the printed range disagree, it asks you to read the report again.
- **`result` and `labread` keep figures as they were given.** The tool cannot know whether a figure was typed or read correctly. With `labread` the reading of the photo is the assistant's, and the read-back is the safeguard: check every row against the paper before you say yes. A figure it could not read is marked unreadable and left out, never guessed. One call takes one report, with one date and one lab, and sixty rows at most. A result that is not a plain number ("negative", "<0.5") is kept as printed.
- **`trend` sets each result against the range of its own report, and never two reports on one scale.** It says when the unit, the lab or the printed range changed between two results, because then the figures cannot simply be set side by side. It does not say whether a change matters: that is a question for the doctor, and the tool says so. It knows a test by the name it was stored under, so "TSH" and "Thyrotropin" are two tests to it. A result stored with no range has nothing read from it.

## Medicines

- **`med` reads a label out; it does not prescribe from it.** The labels are the ones companies have filed with the United States Food and Drug Administration. FDA says of them that their content "has neither been altered nor verified by FDA" and that they "may not be the labeling on currently distributed products or identical to the labeling that is approved". They are American: the same substance sold where you live may come in other strengths, for other uses, with other warnings, under another name. So the leaflet in your own pack is the authority, and where it differs from what `med` prints, yours is right. One label is kept for each substance, type and route, out of many: another company's label for the same medicine may word things differently. There is no dose in the file, on purpose, and no account of how a medicine works or how it was studied. A brand name is found only if it is an American one. One name can be on packs of quite different medicines: a shop's "Pain Relief" may be paracetamol, ibuprofen or a numbing cream, and well-known brands sell several things under one name. Then `med` lists them and picks none, because only the active ingredient printed on your own pack says which it is. The tool says what it read a name as, and says so when it used only part of what was typed: if that is not your medicine, the answer is not about your medicine. A few hundred packs that hold two kinds of tablet are missing, because FDA's data does not say what is in them. What a label says a medicine is for is not advice to take it.
- **The Polish register says what a product is, and nothing about how to take it.** With the Poland pack installed, `med` looks a name up in the national register of medicines first: the product, the substance, strength, form, and whether a pack is sold with or without a prescription. It is the register's state on the day before it was fetched, and the register changes daily: a product may have been added or withdrawn since, and a pack older than a month says so. The substance is named in Latin, as the register has it, and the tool does not turn it into English: Voss does, and says that he did. There is no dose in it and no leaflet text, only a link to the leaflet the register holds. Where an American label is printed after it, that label is about the substance as sold in the United States, not about the Polish product.
- **`sidefx` searches a label for a word.** It looks through the label's side effects, warnings, boxed warning and text for patients for the words you give, and prints the sentences that hold them. It does not know that "itching" is "pruritus" on a label: try both. It cannot tell you that the medicine caused your symptom, and a symptom that is not on the label is not ruled out. Frequencies are the label's, from the company's own studies. Triage still comes first, and whether to carry on with a medicine is for the prescriber or the pharmacist.
- **`interact` and `watch` pass on a grade; they do not judge a combination.** They read pairs of medicines and the grade DDInter 2.0 gives each pair (Major, Moderate, Minor, or none). That is all DDInter lets anyone download, so there is no account of why two medicines interact and none of what to do about it: that question goes to a pharmacist or a prescriber, with the grade. Three things make "not listed" worthless as reassurance, and the tools say so every time. The file is partial: a pair is in it only if one of its two medicines belongs to certain groups of the ATC classification, so two medicines for the heart, two for the nervous system or two against infections are not there at all. Where the files prove that both medicines are outside those groups, the pair is reported as "out of reach" and not as unlisted; where they cannot prove it, an unlisted pair may still be one the file could never have held. It knows nothing of doses, of food, drink or herbs it cannot name, or of the person's kidneys, liver, age or pregnancy. And DDInter itself warns that the absence of an interaction does not mean there is none. Its makers read the literature up to May 2023, so nothing found out since is in it. A grade is about two substances, not about you. Names are a weak point: a medicine is found under DDInter's name or under another name taken from Wikidata, matched by spelling and not checked one by one. The output says what each name was read as; if that is not your medicine, the answer is not about your medicine. A brand name it does not know is reported as unknown and is checked against nothing.
- **`schedule` repeats a schedule it was given.** It never sets one, never tidies one, and does not know whether a dose is right. Dose times are written into the calendar without a time zone, so 08:00 stays 08:00 where you are; a calendar application that insists on a zone will use its own. That means the reminders follow the phone's clock when you travel: right for most tablets, wrong for anything taken at a strict interval, so ask the pharmacist or the prescriber how to carry such a medicine across time zones. Anyone who can see the calendar can read the entries, and a phone may show them on its lock screen: `--discreet` leaves the names out. The file is never written into a git working tree or into the skill's own folder.
- **`refill` is arithmetic on that schedule.** It assumes every scheduled dose was taken, from the day the pack was opened and counting that whole day, because it cannot know otherwise. Missed doses, extra doses and a second pack are invisible to it. Where the sums do not fit (the pack should have run out already) it says so and stops. It uses this machine's clock unless you pass `--now`, and a remote machine's clock may be hours from yours. When it matters, count the pack.
- **`boxread` compares; it does not identify.** The assistant reads the box, and the tool sets the name, the substance and the strength beside the Polish register. Without the Poland pack nothing is checked, and it says so. It compares spellings: a substance read off the box in English may not match the register's Latin name although the two are the same, and a mismatch is a reason to read the box again, not a finding. A name the register does not have may be a gap in the copy or a misreading. A part that could not be read is never guessed, and the box in your hand wins over any file.
- **`cabinet` counts days; it knows nothing about a medicine.** An expiry date is the maker's, and one given as a month runs to the last day of that month. A medicine that got too warm or too damp may be spoilt long before its date, and the tool cannot know. "Open too long" is only worked out where the pack's own label said how long it keeps once opened, and that was stored. "Twice" means the Polish register gives two names on the list the same substance: a product bought abroad, or typed differently from the box, is not compared, and the tool lists those as not checked. The cabinet is the household's list, not one person's: everybody who uses the file sees all of it, with whose each medicine is, so a medicine nobody else should know of does not belong on it.

## What you keep, and the pages made from it

- **`diary` keeps your words.** The date, the symptom, how bad you rated it from 0 to 10, what helped. Nothing in it is checked, weighed or interpreted, and a pattern in it is a question for a doctor.
- **Nothing is saved that was not read back.** `diary`, `result`, `labread`, `cabinet`, `kit` and `pet` print what they would store and a code, and write only when the same command comes back with that code; a different change, a changed file or another day gives another code. That makes the read-back hard to skip. It cannot make anyone listen to it.
- **`card`, `passport` and `brief` print what is on file, in your words.** Only the headings are translated, into English, Polish or both; an entry stays as you gave it, so a doctor abroad may not be able to read it. The Polish headings are this skill's wording and no official form. A page is not a medical record, and its last line says so. It is written only after you have seen exactly that page, and never into a git working tree or into the skill's own folder.
- **Anyone who is handed a page can read it.** So can anyone who picks up a phone with a medical ID on its lock screen. That is what such a page is for, and why you choose each line. The tool writes a text file laid out for the phone's own emergency screen; putting it there is yours to do.
- **`kit` lists what you told it is in a kit.** It flags what has expired by the dates you gave. What a kit ought to hold is not on file. What the law asks for in a car comes from the Poland pack, for Poland.
- **`carer` shows the records of the people in someone's care.** The note on consent is what was written down; the tool cannot know whether the person really agreed, or still does. A record with no questions of its own is guarded by the carer's gate, which is a soft lock. Nothing is on file about help for carers.
- **`round` is a list of what is due, for those who switched it on.** Refills by the schedule's arithmetic (with `refill`'s assumption that every dose was taken), the visits on file, expiry dates from the cabinet and the kits, and what has gone stale. It leaves out the things a person keeps to themselves, so that a round can be read where others see it. It is off unless switched on in the person's file, or asked for. "Weekly" is a habit, not a timer.
- **`desk`, `season` and `checkin` are notices a person has to switch on.** Off means none is given. There are no figures on file for breaks or water and no dates for vaccinations or the weather, so the tool gives none and tells Voss to cite what he looks up or leave it out. "One notice, and no more" is his rule to keep: the tool does not count. A check-in is a question and a listen. It is not a screening, it has no score, and any sign of crisis goes to the clinical rules at once.
- **`translate` checks figures, not meaning.** The translating is the assistant's. The tool compares the figures of the original with those of the translation, as written, and counts the terms it was told to keep. It is strict on purpose: a decimal comma turned into a point, or a date written another way, counts as a change. It cannot tell a wrong word from a right one, or notice a missing "not". A number written out in words is not a figure to it. It does not print the figures themselves, because one may be somebody's result. It works on text: a scan has to be read first, and what was read wrongly is compared wrongly.
- **`recipe` and `scale` are baking, not advice.** The four recipes were written for this skill and nobody has baked them from this file yet: they say "untested" until someone has. The allergens are read off the list of ingredients, which is no promise about a kitchen. `scale` multiplies weights exactly; the tin and the baking time are for the recipe as written and do not scale with it.

## The personal file

- **The gate is a soft lock.** `ignition` judges a reply against the answers on file, forgiving case, accents, word order and small typos, and never prints one. It judges and does nothing more: the other commands do not check that it was passed, nothing counts failed attempts, and nothing stops an assistant from opening the file. So keeping a file shut until then, keeping to three questions, and not looking are Voss's rules to follow. That stops a file being read aloud to the wrong person in a conversation. It is not encryption: the file is plain JSON, and anyone who can open the package can read it. Once passed, a gate stays open for the conversation.
- **Nobody is turned away at the gate.** Someone who does not pass it gets the same care and the same knowledge, only without the personal file. There is no way round the gate for an emergency, and none is needed: the emergency answer is the number to call, and it never depended on the file.
- **`profile` reads back what you said, as you said it.** Ages are worked out from the date of birth on the day you ask; a year alone leaves a year's doubt, and the line says so. "Stale" notes (a profile nobody has changed for over a year, a pregnancy status over thirty days old, medicines told over a year ago, travel over three months ago) are reminders to ask again. Nothing is deleted for being old. `boot` shows the day the file was last changed, which also tells you whether the copy that is loaded is the one you saved last.
- **`intake` and `rebuild` store only what was read back.** The code `intake` prints stands for exactly the file that would result; a different change, a changed file or a different day gives a different code, and `rebuild` then refuses. That makes the read-back hard to skip. It cannot make anyone listen to it.
- **The check for numbers that are never stored is a net, not a promise.** It recognises payment card numbers, IBANs, US social security numbers written with their hyphens, and two national number formats by their check digits, plus a few tell-tale words. A number in another shape will get past it. Refusing those is still Voss's rule.
- **`forget` removes from the new file only.** The field, entry or profile is gone from the file it writes: no empty slot, no note that it was there. The package or file you used until then still holds it, and so do backups and anything already said in a conversation. Delete the old copies yourself.
- **`rebuild` writes a private file.** A rebuilt package has your personal file in it, and the household file if your copy has one. The tool refuses to write it into a git working tree or into the package it is running from. Where you keep it after that is up to you.

## The toolkit itself

- **`boot` shows this machine's clock**, which may not be yours: a remote machine can be hours away. `refill` and `round` take `--now` for that reason. Everything else that counts days (an age, an expiry, a stale note) counts them on the machine's date.
- **`theatre` prints a banner and the three guardrails.** Switching mode changes nothing in the tools. That real medicines keep their real rules on stage, and that a real symptom ends the scene, are his rules to keep.
- **`tools --find` matches words.** It looks for your words in what each command says it does and in a few more words listed for it. It can miss, or name the wrong command: `tools` lists them all.
- **`check --reply` checks form.** Typography, the marker line, ear cues, a banner written wrongly, a few phrases that are never said, and length. It cannot tell whether a claim is sourced, whether the tone suits the person, or whether a reply is kind.
- **`selftest` checks the toolkit on this machine**: known answers for the date sums, the refill sums, the lab comparison, the calendar format and the gate; that the data files and pictures are present; that each database says where it came from and that its packed text can be read by this Python; and that the personal file, if there is one, reads and has nothing to fix. It says where a problem is and never quotes what is written there.
- **Voss himself is fiction.** The android, the fur and the cyan eyes are a character, and so are his senses: he has not seen you, listened to your chest or felt your pulse, and he does not say that he has. What he knows of you is what you told him and what you showed him. The rules he follows are real.
